# From regional signals to a one-page policy brief

The scan collects observations. The brief makes a small number of supported arguments about changes that could affect people's opportunities, services, income or choices.

The process is: **fix the evidence window, compare mechanisms, develop arguments, test the logic, rewrite in plain language, approve the text, then format the page.**

## 1. Set the question and freeze the evidence window

Name the intended reader, the question the brief helps them consider, the places covered and the cutoff date. Use the cumulative register to select a bounded period. Keep the source records unchanged while drafting; later developments belong in a subsequent version.

Report the count of unique signals and material updates only if verified. It describes the collection, not the strength of the conclusions. Separate observations, linked updates and analytical products. Six focal countries do not represent the whole region.

For an exploratory brief, the purpose may be to identify consequential questions rather than recommend actions. Do not add a policy-priorities section unless the brief's purpose calls for one. The exercise this method draws on deliberately removed that section.

## 2. Group by the mechanism, then choose the arguments

Review the underlying observations and counterevidence across countries. Group items by what could change: a barrier to entering work, the responsibility for resolving a service problem, the route from expenditure to income, or the contract through which a firm earns revenue.

Themes help organise the evidence but are not yet arguments. “AI and employment” names a topic. “Employers may demand experience while reducing the work through which beginners acquire it” identifies a tension to investigate.

Choose three or four distinct arguments for one page. Prefer a concrete small observation with a clear mechanism over a large announcement with speculative effects. Split a section when its examples require different causal chains. For example, infrastructure finance and software billing contracts may both concern economic returns, but each deserves its own argument if their connection cannot be explained clearly.

## 3. Build an argument card before writing prose

Use one card per proposed section:

| Element | What to record |
| --- | --- |
| Proposition | One sentence naming a specific change in barriers, responsibilities, incentives, bargaining power or the distribution of gains |
| Observations | One to three concrete examples, each with a canonical ID, original source, date and exact geographic scope |
| Mechanism | The steps that would connect those observations to the proposition |
| Missing link | What has not been observed and must remain conditional |
| Competing outcomes | At least two plausible directions, with the conditions that would distinguish them |
| Distribution | Who might gain access, earn income, carry work or bear risk; distinguish observation from hypothesis |
| Challenge | The strongest alternative explanation or counterexample, and what would weaken the proposition |

An anecdote can reveal a mechanism worth investigating. It cannot establish national prevalence. Preserve qualifiers such as reported, proposed, conditional or piloted. Do not turn capacity, training places or automated contacts into evidence of employment, productivity or successful service resolution.

## 4. Test the causal logic with questions

For every argument, ask:

1. What exactly happened, and what does the source establish?
2. What would have to happen next for the proposed effect to follow?
3. Which of those links are observed, inferred or still unknown?
4. What else could explain the observation?
5. Who gains time, income, access or discretion, and who takes on work or risk?
6. Under what conditions could the opposite outcome occur?
7. What observation would make us revise the argument?
8. Does the evidence support this geographic scope, or only a case within it?

Write the answers in working notes first. Then turn the reasoning into connected prose. The final page need not be a list of questions. Each sentence should give the reader a reason to accept the next step.

## 5. Audit sentences for strategic value

Use this editorial scale to diagnose the draft. It is not a measure of evidence quality and does not belong on the final page.

| Level | Sentence function | Editorial response |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Describes a fact | Keep the necessary evidence, with attribution and scope |
| 2 | States general relevance | Replace generic benefits/risks with a specific connection, or delete |
| 3 | Explains a mechanism | Check that the intermediate steps are explicit and supported or conditional |
| 4 | Explains a strategic implication | Specify the change in opportunity, obligation, incentives or bargaining power |
| 5 | Offers a supported insight that changes understanding | Test the surprising connection particularly carefully; do not manufacture one |

A good brief needs factual sentences. Do not inflate every sentence into a grand claim. Remove phrases that merely announce importance. Check words such as widening, accelerating and shifting against evidence over time. Two examples do not establish a trend. A disclaimer at the end does not repair an overstatement in the body.

Ask whether the paragraph could appear unchanged in a brief on almost any technology. If it could, replace it with the specific mechanism shown by the selected evidence.

## 6. Write the page as connected arguments

Use a short title, period and geographic scope, followed by three or four sections. Each section normally has:

- A heading that expresses the argument rather than naming a broad topic.
- A short opening proposition in ordinary language.
- One or two sentences containing concrete, attributed examples.
- A compact explanation of the mechanism, competing outcomes and their consequences for people or institutions.
- A line of links: **Supporting signals [1] [2] [3]**. Each number links directly to the source supporting that section.

Make the stakes understandable through people's lives: entering a profession, resolving a difficult service problem, earning from a business or retaining income locally. Avoid slogans, imagined certainty and generic claims that technology will transform everything. Expand unfamiliar abbreviations at first use.

Keep financial concepts distinct. A financing ceiling is not money disbursed. Spending is not productivity. Tax receipts are not total domestic value captured. If currencies are converted, retain the rate, date and basis in working notes; do not introduce an unexplained conversion for rhetorical effect.

End with a two-sentence method note explaining multilingual discovery, source comparison and counterevidence, followed by the limits of the interpretation. A useful model is: “Multilingual scanning identified the observations; comparison of original sources and counterevidence informed the arguments. These are emerging issues and competing possibilities, not estimates of national prevalence or a single regional forecast.”

## 7. Approve the wording before designing the artifact

Show the complete text before editing the final presentation or document. Revise the reasoning in text until the author approves it. Then freeze that version. Layout is a separate step, and fitting a page is not permission to change an approved argument.

For the one-page A4 version used in the originating exercise, four full-width sections made the arguments easier to follow. This is an option, not a universal template. Keep type readable; do not solve overflow by shrinking it excessively. Return to the author if meaningful cuts are needed.

Verify the exported artifact, not just the source file: one A4 page, approved wording intact, no clipping or overlaps, every numbered source link clickable and pointing to the intended source. Keep a full source list or evidence ledger outside the page. The public kit includes a Markdown template; it does not automate document or presentation export.

## What this method produces

A short evidence-based interpretation for discussion. It preserves uncertainty while explaining the mechanisms that could shape decisions. It does not turn a collection of signals into representative statistics, a causal impact evaluation or an approved policy programme.
